### Gut microbiota diversity and composition are weakly associated with breeding phenology in wild blue tits
1.	In seasonal breeding birds, late-season reproduction in temperate regions often results in smaller clutches and reduced offspring survival, primarily due to declining environmental conditions. While the fitness consequences of reproductive timing are well documented, its association with the gut microbiota, an important determinant of health and fitness, remains underexplored. 
2.	Addressing this knowledge gap is increasingly important, as climate change is expected to shift breeding phenology. This includes changes in food sources and their abundance, which may affect gut microbiota. 
3.	To assess whether avian fitness is linked to gut microbiota, we studied wild blue tits (Cyanistes caeruleus), a model species with a time-sensitive dependence on Lepidoptera larvae, across the breeding season. We used cloacal swabs as proxies for gut microbiota from early- and late-breeding females and their chicks. 
4.	For females, egg-laying date was positively associated with Inverse Simpson diversity, but not with other diversity indices. In chicks, Shannon and Inverse Simpson diversity were negatively associated with hatching date. Chick gut microbiota composition also shifted over the season towards a higher prevalence of taxa from the Enterobacterales order. 
5.	Exploratory metagenomics analysis showed that early-breeding females had enriched fatty acid synthesis pathways compared to late-breeding females, potentially due to dietary differences. 
6.	These findings suggest that phenological effects in females may be overshadowed by inter-individual variation or differences in habitat quality. While hatching time was correlated with alpha-diversity and gut microbiota composition in chicks, we need to further explore the functional implications of these microbial shifts and their causal relationships with host health.
